Act II · The stakes

Now the audience is an AI.

6 of 6 major U.S. AI developers train on your chats by default. Stanford HAI, 2025

Your drafts, your late-night questions, the things you'd never post. Some companies keep them indefinitely. Some let people read the transcripts.

Find the switch.

When one major lab changed its terms in 2025, the training toggle sat under a big "Accept" button, already on. Leave it on, and your chats can be kept for up to five years.

Switched on before you got here. The giant one on stage works too.

Now do it for real: tonight, open every chatbot you use and find this switch.

Ask Tristan Buckmaster.

The NYU mathematician kept unpublished research in an AI coding tool. When the company said its AI had cracked the problem he works on, he asked whether it had seen his drafts. Their answer:

"While unlikely, we cannot rule out that de-identified data derived from their usage of our products helped improve our models."

Once your ideas are in the cloud, even the company can't say where they went.

Why the show keeps running

  1. Money. Every conversation makes the next model sharper, so your chats are worth keeping.
  2. Law. No federal privacy law. 24 states wrote their own; of 49 federal bills, two narrow ones passed.
  3. Design. Pre-switched toggles and giant "Accept" buttons work, which is why they're everywhere.

Act IV · Take it back

There's a trapdoor out of this show.

At M3 and SNAP Life Sciences, I worked with pharmaceutical and financial data too valuable to hand to anyone else. On a startup budget, the answer was local AI: models running on our own hardware. It cost less, and the data stayed ours.

Try it. It's already here.

This model runs on your own graphics chip. Ask it about the argument, or paste a privacy policy and let it pull out the parts about training, how long they keep your data, and who reads it. Then watch the request counter.

An AI that never leaves this device

Qwen3.5 0.8B, running in your browser. It downloads once (447 MB) and stays on this device. After that, nothing you type goes anywhere.

"But free AI needs our data to get better."

Then ask for it. Make training opt-in for everyone, the way enterprise customers already have it, and let the people who want to contribute choose to.

Your move.

  1. Tonight: turn off chat training in every AI tool you use.
  2. This week: turn on Global Privacy Control, so your browser tells every site not to sell your data.
  3. From now on: keep sensitive work on a local model, like the one above, or free apps such as Ollama and LM Studio.
  4. Speak up: tell your representatives you want a federal privacy law that sets a floor for every state.
  5. Build it: HACK1984, a 24-hour privacy-first hackathon in Boston, Nov 7–8.

The next time a pop-up asks for your consent, remember it's a performance, and ask yourself one question.

Who do you trust with your most sensitive data?
