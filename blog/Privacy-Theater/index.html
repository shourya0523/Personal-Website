Act I · The performance

Your software is watching you.

Open a website, any website, and before you can even find the "Reject All" button, it already knows your device, your city, your internet provider and a lot more. This one is no different.

Look at the stage. Those cards are leaving your seat.

56% of Americans routinely click "agree" without reading.

61% doubt anything they do will make a difference. Pew Research Center, 2023

Most of us have learned to play our part without reading the script, and that resignation is the theater working exactly as designed, since a choice that feels pointless is a choice people stop making.

The banner on stage works. Choose, and watch what changes.

Act II · The stakes

The cookie banner has a successor.

6 of 6 major U.S. AI developers train on your chats by default. Stanford HAI, 2025

For years the stakes felt small, a few targeted ads and an eerie sense of being followed around the internet. Now your drafts, your questions and the things you would never post become training data by default, with some companies keeping them indefinitely and some letting people read the transcripts.

Find the switch.

When one major lab updated its terms in 2025, users saw a large "Accept" button with a much smaller training toggle beneath it, already switched on, and anyone who left it on can now have their chats kept for up to five years.

Switched on before you got here. The giant one on stage works too.

Now find the real one, tonight, in every chatbot you use.

Ask Tristan Buckmaster.

Buckmaster, a mathematician at NYU, worked through unpublished research in an AI coding tool, and when the company announced its AI had solved the famous Navier-Stokes problem, a claim mathematicians are still examining, he asked a simple question: had the model seen his drafts? Their answer deserves to be read slowly:

"While unlikely, we cannot rule out that de-identified data derived from their usage of our products helped improve our models."

Once your ideas enter a cloud model, even the company running it cannot fully trace where they go.

Act III · The price

Privacy, sold separately.

The same update that switched the toggle on for everyday users left business, enterprise and API customers untouched. Privacy, therefore, is already for sale, and the companies that can afford it are buying. Everyone else pays with their conversations.

"If you are not paying for the AI you use with your data, you are probably paying with it." Me, on LinkedIn

Three forces keep the show running.

  1. Money. Data has become the fuel for training AI, and every conversation makes the next model a little sharper.
  2. Law. The US still has no comprehensive federal privacy law. 24 states wrote their own; of 49 federal bills, two narrow ones passed.
  3. Design. A pre-switched toggle and a giant "Accept" button do exactly what they were built to do, and most of us have stopped pushing back.

Act IV · Take it back

The other side of the coin.

I learned how much private AI matters by building it. At M3 and SNAP Life Sciences, I worked with pharmaceutical and financial data where everything was valuable and proprietary, and with a startup's resources, that meant finding creative ways to run privacy-first, local AI. A model running on your own hardware keeps your sharpest ideas exactly where they belong, so the savings and the privacy arrive together.

There's a trapdoor out of this show.

The model below runs on your own graphics chip, so you can paste in a privacy policy and have it pull out what they train on, how long they keep it and who gets to read it, or ask it about the argument, all while the request counter stays at zero.

"But free AI needs our data to get better."

To be sure, user data does make models better, and free tools have bills to pay. Regardless, the burden of protecting that data belongs with the companies collecting it, so training should stay off by default for everyone, the same protection enterprise customers already enjoy without lifting a finger.

Your move.

The fixes are within reach, and the first few start with you, tonight.

  1. Tonight: turn off chat training in every AI tool you use.
  2. This week: turn on Global Privacy Control, so your browser tells every site not to sell your data.
  3. From now on: keep sensitive work on a local model, like the one above, or free apps such as Ollama and LM Studio.
  4. Speak up: tell your representatives you want a federal privacy law that sets a floor for every state and lets them build higher, and tell AI companies that training should stay off unless you opt in.
  5. Build it: HACK1984, a 24-hour privacy-first hackathon in Boston, Nov 7–8.

The next time a pop-up asks for your consent, remember you're watching a performance, and ask yourself the question I asked on LinkedIn.

Who do you trust with your most sensitive data?
